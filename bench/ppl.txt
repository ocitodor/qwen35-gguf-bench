The spread of printing with movable type changed how knowledge moved through Europe in the second half of the fifteenth century. Before the press, a book was copied by hand, page by page, and a single large volume could occupy a scribe for the better part of a year. Copies were expensive, scarce, and prone to errors that accumulated as each generation of copyists worked from an already imperfect exemplar. The press reversed all three conditions at once. A compositor set a page of type once, a pressman could pull several hundred impressions of it in a day, and every impression was identical to every other. Within fifty years of the first printed books, presses were operating in more than two hundred towns, and the number of volumes in circulation had grown from tens of thousands to many millions.

The technical ingredients were not new in themselves. Paper had reached Europe from the Islamic world centuries earlier. Screw presses were used for wine and olive oil. Metalworkers knew how to cast small objects in moulds. What was new was the combination: an alloy of lead, tin and antimony that melted at a low temperature and cooled without shrinking, a hand mould that let a caster produce hundreds of identical letters in an hour, an oil-based ink that clung to metal rather than beading on it, and a press adapted so that a flat platen pressed paper evenly onto a bed of inked type. Each part had to work with the others, and the people who assembled the system were as much businessmen as craftsmen, borrowing heavily to finance years of experiments before a saleable book appeared.

Early printers imitated manuscripts closely. Their type was cut to resemble the formal book hands of the day, their pages left room for hand-painted initials and decorations, and buyers sometimes could not tell a printed book from a written one at first glance. Over the following decades the printed book found its own conventions: title pages, page numbers, running heads, indexes, and the smaller, rounder letterforms that remain familiar today. These conventions made books easier to navigate and compare, and they made it possible for a reader in one city to refer a reader in another to a precise passage, confident that both were looking at the same words on the same page.

Bread rises because of gas trapped in an elastic network of protein. When flour is mixed with water, two proteins in wheat, glutenin and gliadin, absorb the water and begin to link into long chains. Kneading stretches and aligns these chains, folding them over one another until they form a continuous, stretchy web that bakers call gluten. The web is strong enough to hold a shape but flexible enough to expand, which is exactly what is needed to capture the gas that yeast produces.

Yeast is a single-celled fungus that lives on sugar. Flour contains little free sugar, but it contains starch, and enzymes present in the flour begin breaking the starch into simpler sugars as soon as water is added. The yeast consumes these sugars and releases carbon dioxide and a small amount of alcohol. The carbon dioxide cannot escape through the gluten web, so it collects in thousands of tiny bubbles. As fermentation continues, the bubbles grow, the dough swells to double or triple its original size, and the alcohol and other byproducts develop the aromas that distinguish a slowly risen loaf from a hurried one.

Temperature controls the pace. Yeast works slowly in a cold kitchen and quickly in a warm one, and above a certain point it dies. Bakers use this to their advantage. A dough left overnight in a refrigerator rises slowly and develops a more complex flavour, while a dough placed in a warm cupboard is ready to shape within an hour or two. Salt also moderates fermentation, tightening the gluten and slowing the yeast slightly, which is one reason that bread made without salt tends to rise fast, collapse, and taste flat.

In the oven, the final transformation happens quickly. The heat drives the yeast into a last burst of activity and expands the trapped gas, so the loaf springs upward in the first few minutes of baking. Then the proteins set, the starches gelatinise, and the structure becomes permanent. On the surface, sugars and proteins react to form the brown crust and its characteristic smell. The alcohol produced during fermentation evaporates almost entirely. What remains is a lattice of baked gluten surrounding the holes where bubbles once were, which is why a slice of good bread looks like a cross-section of foam.

Ocean tides are the visible result of a gravitational tug-of-war between the Earth, the Moon and the Sun. The Moon pulls on the near side of the Earth more strongly than on the far side, because gravity weakens with distance. The water on the near side is drawn toward the Moon and heaps up into a bulge. Less intuitively, the water on the far side is pulled less than the planet's centre is, so it is in effect left behind, forming a second bulge opposite the first. As the Earth rotates beneath these two bulges, most coastlines pass through two high tides and two low tides in a little over twenty-four hours.

The Sun exerts a similar but weaker influence, roughly half as strong as the Moon's, because although the Sun is enormously more massive it is also very much farther away. When the Sun and Moon line up, at new moon and full moon, their effects add together and produce the large spring tides. When they stand at right angles to each other, at the quarter moons, their effects partly cancel and the result is the smaller neap tides. The cycle from spring to neap and back again takes about two weeks and is one of the most reliable rhythms in nature.

Local geography shapes what any particular shore actually experiences. A funnel-shaped bay can concentrate the tidal bulge until the difference between high and low water exceeds ten metres, while an enclosed sea with a narrow entrance may barely register a tide at all. The shape of a sea floor can delay the tide by hours, so that neighbouring ports have their high water at noticeably different times. Rivers that meet the sea carry the tide far inland, and in a few places the incoming water forms a single breaking wave that travels upstream for many kilometres.

For people who live and work by the sea, tides are practical facts before they are scientific ones. A harbour that is deep enough at high water may be a mudflat six hours later. Fishing, shipping, shellfish gathering and coastal construction are all planned around tide tables, which were among the earliest published scientific predictions and remain among the most accurate. Modern tables are computed from decades of measurements at each port and account for dozens of separate periodic components, but the dominant terms are still the ones that a careful observer could have inferred from watching the Moon.

The first railways were not built for passengers. They were built to move coal and stone from mines and quarries to the nearest river or canal, using wooden and later iron rails along which horses pulled wagons with far less effort than on an ordinary road. The idea of replacing the horse with a steam engine took shape in the early nineteenth century, when engines had become light and reliable enough to move themselves as well as a load. The decisive demonstration came when a line connecting two industrial towns opened with locomotives hauling both freight and paying passengers, and the passengers turned out to be more profitable than anyone had anticipated.

What followed was a building boom of a scale that had no precedent. Within a few decades, thousands of kilometres of track had been laid across Britain, continental Europe and North America. Companies competed to connect cities, raised enormous sums from investors, and employed armies of labourers who cut through hills, bridged valleys and tunnelled under mountains with picks, shovels and gunpowder. Many lines were poorly planned and some companies collapsed, but the network that survived carried goods and people at speeds that would have seemed fantastical a generation earlier.

Railways altered time itself in a practical sense. Before them, each town kept its own local time, set by the sun, and a journey of a hundred kilometres could cross several minutes of difference. Timetables made this intolerable, since a train could not be scheduled against a dozen local clocks. Railway companies adopted a single standard time for their whole network, towns along the line followed, and national standard time, later organised into global time zones, grew directly out of this requirement.

The social effects were just as far-reaching. Fresh food could reach cities from distant farms before it spoiled. Newspapers printed in the capital could be read in provincial towns the same day. Workers could live farther from their jobs, and seaside resorts grew up at the ends of lines to serve city dwellers on holiday. Armies moved by rail, and the strategic value of a railway network became a central concern of governments. For roughly a century, until the motor car and the aeroplane matured, the railway was the dominant means of long-distance travel on land.

A honeybee colony behaves in many ways like a single organism. Tens of thousands of individuals cooperate so closely in gathering food, regulating temperature, raising young and defending the hive that biologists sometimes describe the colony rather than the bee as the fundamental unit. Almost all of the bees in a hive are female workers, which live for a few weeks in summer and progress through a sequence of jobs as they age: cleaning cells, feeding larvae, building comb, receiving and storing nectar, guarding the entrance, and finally foraging outside.

The queen is the only fertile female and may lay more than a thousand eggs a day at the height of the season. She does not rule in any meaningful sense; her influence is chemical, through pheromones that signal her presence and health to the workers. If she weakens or dies, the workers detect the change within hours and begin raising new queens by feeding selected larvae a rich diet. Males, called drones, exist only to mate with queens from other colonies and are expelled from the hive as autumn approaches.

Foraging bees communicate the location of flowers through a repeated figure-of-eight movement on the vertical comb, known as the waggle dance. The angle of the straight run relative to vertical corresponds to the direction of the food relative to the sun, and the duration of the run encodes the distance. Other bees follow the dancer, pick up the scent of the flowers she visited, and fly out to find them. The system allows a colony to concentrate its effort on the richest sources within several kilometres and to shift that effort quickly as different plants come into bloom.

Temperature regulation is a collective achievement. The brood area is kept close to thirty-five degrees Celsius regardless of weather. In cold conditions, workers cluster tightly and generate heat by shivering their flight muscles. In hot conditions, they spread out, fan their wings to move air through the hive, and spread droplets of water on the comb so that evaporation cools it. Honey, which is nectar concentrated by evaporation and transformed by enzymes, is the colony's stored energy for winter, when no flowers are available and the cluster must generate warmth for months from reserves gathered in summer.

Glass is made by melting sand with substances that lower its melting point and stabilise the result. Pure silica melts at a temperature far beyond what ancient furnaces could reach, so early glassmakers added soda, obtained from plant ashes or natural deposits, which brought the melting point down to a manageable level. Soda alone produced a glass that slowly dissolved in water, so lime was added as well, usually without the makers understanding why it helped. The resulting soda-lime glass is still the most common type produced today, used for windows, bottles and jars.

The earliest glass objects were small beads and vessels formed around a core of clay or sand that was scraped out after the glass cooled. The invention of glassblowing, in which a worker gathers molten glass on the end of a hollow tube and inflates it, transformed the craft. Blowing made it possible to produce thin-walled vessels quickly, in a wide range of shapes, and at a price that ordinary households could afford. Workshops sprang up throughout the Roman world, and glass became an everyday material rather than a luxury.

Flat glass for windows was harder to make. For centuries the main methods were to blow a large bubble and spin it into a disc, or to blow a cylinder, cut it lengthwise and flatten it in a kiln. Both produced panes that were small, uneven and slightly distorted. Plate glass, cast onto a table and laboriously ground and polished, was clearer but enormously expensive. The modern float process, in which molten glass is poured onto a bath of molten tin and spreads into a perfectly flat ribbon, dates only from the middle of the twentieth century and made large, flawless windows routine.

Colour in glass comes from small amounts of metal oxides dissolved in the melt. Iron, present as an impurity in most sand, gives the faint green tint visible at the edge of an ordinary window pane. Cobalt produces deep blue, copper gives turquoise or red depending on the furnace conditions, manganese yields purple, and gold in minute quantities produces a rich ruby. Removing colour was as much of an art as adding it, and the clear, colourless glass that we now take for granted was for a long time the most prized and difficult kind to make.

Weather at mid-latitudes is dominated by the meeting of air masses with different temperatures and moisture. Cold, dense air from polar regions and warm, humid air from the tropics do not mix readily. Where they meet, a boundary forms, known as a front, and most of the clouds, rain and wind that people experience from day to day are generated along these boundaries as one air mass pushes against or slides over another.

At a warm front, warm air advances over retreating cold air. Being lighter, the warm air rises gradually up the long, shallow slope of the cold air ahead of it. As it rises it cools, its moisture condenses, and a broad sequence of clouds develops: thin, high wisps far ahead of the front, then a thickening layer, and finally low, grey cloud with steady rain that may last for many hours. The passage of the front brings a rise in temperature and a shift in wind direction, often followed by a period of mild, damp weather.

At a cold front, cold air advances and undercuts the warm air ahead of it. The slope is steeper and the warm air is forced upward quickly, producing tall cumulus clouds, heavy showers and sometimes thunderstorms along a relatively narrow band. The front usually passes faster than a warm front does, and behind it the air is cooler, drier and clearer, with the sky often filled with scattered, well-defined clouds and good visibility.

These fronts are typically parts of larger rotating systems. A low-pressure centre draws air inward and upward, with a warm front extending ahead of it and a cold front trailing behind. The whole system drifts eastward in the prevailing winds, so that an observer at a fixed point experiences the sequence of warm front, warm sector, cold front and clearing skies over a day or two. Forecasting at this scale consists largely of tracking these systems, estimating their speed and intensity, and judging how they will interact with mountains, coasts and other systems in their path.

Public libraries, in the sense of collections open to anyone without charge, are a relatively recent institution. Libraries themselves are ancient, but for most of history they belonged to temples, palaces, monasteries, universities and wealthy individuals, and access depended on membership or patronage. Subscription libraries, in which members paid an annual fee, became popular in the eighteenth century and widened the circle of readers considerably, but they still excluded those who could not afford the fee.

The idea that a town should provide books for all its residents, paid for from local taxes, gained ground in the nineteenth century alongside the spread of elementary schooling. Reformers argued that a population newly able to read needed something worth reading, and that a free library would offer an alternative to the tavern, improve the skills of workers, and strengthen the civic life of the town. Legislation permitting towns to levy a small rate for libraries was followed by a wave of building, often helped by donations from industrialists who saw libraries as a respectable form of philanthropy.

The early public library was a formal and somewhat forbidding place. Books were kept in closed stacks, requested by catalogue number and fetched by staff. Readers consulted them at long tables in silence. Open shelves, where readers browse and choose for themselves, were introduced gradually and were controversial at first, since librarians feared theft and disorder. Children's rooms, lending for home reading, branch libraries in outlying districts and travelling libraries for rural areas followed over the first half of the twentieth century.

Today a public library lends far more than books. Music, films, magazines, newspapers and digital resources are standard, and the building itself often serves as a meeting place, a study space, a venue for classes and a point of access to the internet for people who have none at home. The principle that has endured through all these changes is the original one: that information and the means to use it should be available to every member of a community regardless of their ability to pay.

Every flat map of the Earth is a compromise, because the surface of a sphere cannot be laid out on a plane without stretching or tearing. A map projection is a rule for deciding where that distortion goes. Some projections preserve the shapes of small regions, so that angles measured on the map match angles on the ground, and these were long favoured by navigators because a constant compass bearing appears as a straight line. The price is a steady exaggeration of area toward the poles, so that landmasses in high latitudes look several times larger than their true size relative to those near the equator.

Other projections preserve area, so that any two regions of equal size on the Earth occupy equal space on the map. These are valuable for showing the distribution of population, resources or climate without visual bias, but they achieve their accuracy by distorting shapes, often dramatically near the edges. A third family preserves distance or direction from a single central point, which is useful for showing routes or ranges from a particular city but misleading everywhere else.

No projection can preserve shape, area and distance at the same time over a whole globe, so the choice depends on purpose. A world map intended for a classroom wall might use a compromise projection that balances the various distortions so that nothing looks grotesque, accepting that nothing is quite right either. A map for a hiker covering a few dozen kilometres can ignore the problem entirely, since over such a small area the Earth is flat for all practical purposes and any reasonable projection will do.

The problem becomes concrete when maps are joined. A country mapped in a series of sheets must use a projection that fits its shape and position, and neighbouring countries often choose differently, so that sheets from either side of a border do not align. International efforts to standardise projections and reference systems have reduced these difficulties, and the global positioning systems in everyday use rely on a single mathematical model of the Earth's shape, but the fundamental impossibility of a perfect flat map remains as true as it ever was.

People have measured time by the sky for as long as there have been people. The day is given by the rotation of the Earth, the month by the phases of the Moon, and the year by the cycle of the seasons. Dividing the day into smaller parts required instruments. The sundial, which casts a shadow onto a marked surface, is the oldest and in some respects the most reliable, since it reads the sun directly, but it is useless at night and in cloudy weather. Water clocks, which measure the steady flow of water from one vessel into another, filled the gap and were refined over centuries into elaborate devices with floats, gears and figures that struck bells.

The mechanical clock appeared in Europe in the late Middle Ages. Its essential innovation was the escapement, a device that allows a falling weight to turn a wheel in a series of small, regular steps rather than all at once. Early mechanical clocks were large, housed in towers, and so inaccurate that they were reset daily by the sun. They had no dials at first and announced the hours by bells, which were enough to call people to work, prayer and market in a town where few needed to know the time more precisely than that.

Accuracy improved by stages. The pendulum, whose swing takes nearly the same time regardless of its amplitude, gave clocks a natural regulator and reduced errors from minutes a day to seconds. The balance spring did the same for portable watches. The quest for a clock that would keep time at sea, where a pendulum is useless, drove a century of invention and was finally rewarded with a timekeeper that could cross an ocean and lose only a few seconds, allowing navigators to calculate their longitude by comparing local noon with the time at a known reference point.

Modern timekeeping has left mechanical oscillators behind. A quartz crystal vibrates at a precise frequency when a voltage is applied, and counting those vibrations gives a watch that is accurate to a few seconds a month at a cost of almost nothing. Atomic clocks measure the frequency of radiation absorbed by atoms and are so stable that they would lose less than a second in millions of years. The world's civil time is now defined by an ensemble of such clocks, with occasional adjustments to keep it aligned with the slightly irregular rotation of the planet whose spin first gave us the idea of a day.
